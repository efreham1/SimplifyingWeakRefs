\section{Interpretation of Results}
\label{sec:discussion-interpretation}

The combination of the specialised clear path and the dynamic array achieves a substantially greater reduction in non-strong processing time than either optimisation individually. In the single-object benchmark, the combined variants reduce the median by about 81\%, compared with 7\% for \texttt{clear\_path\_only} and 36\% for \texttt{dyn\_only}. The two mechanisms address different parts of the cost. The dynamic array improves traversal locality by replacing pointer chasing with sequential scanning~\cite{drepper07-memory}. The specialised clear path removes part of the per-reference work once a queue-less weak reference is already being processed on a dedicated fast path. Each optimisation therefore increases the practical value of the other. The near-equivalence of \texttt{clear\_path\_dyn} and \texttt{all} follows from this: once the traversal structure and the per-reference work are both improved, adding the separate-list mechanism yields very little further benefit. The \texttt{sep\_only} variant confirms the same point from the other direction: routing references to a separate list without changing either traversal locality or the per-reference operations yields almost no speedup.

The \texttt{weak\_fields} variant achieves a lower non-strong processing time than the baseline but falls short of \texttt{clear\_path\_dyn}. At the same time, it is by far the strongest configuration on total collection time and heap footprint. This combination suggests that weak fields primarily reduce the \emph{amount} of GC work that the collector has to perform across the whole cycle, whereas the best \texttt{WeakReference} variants primarily reduce the \emph{cost per weak reference} inside one specific subphase.

Despite the substantial reductions in non-strong processing time, the \texttt{WeakReference} optimisations have a limited measurable effect on total collection time. Reference processing accounts for only a fraction of the total GC cycle; the remainder is dominated by concurrent marking, relocation, and other phases that the optimisations do not touch. The overlapping distributions for major collection and old-generation time across \texttt{WeakReference} variants reflect this: once reference processing is no longer the dominant cost, further gains in that subphase translate to at most a small fraction of total cycle time.

The bimodal shape of many violin plots for overall collection metrics suggests that the benchmark encounters two distinct operating regimes. A plausible explanation is that some GC cycles carry a heavier concurrent mark-follow workload than others, perhaps due to object lifetimes or allocation patterns interacting with the timing of the explicit \texttt{System.gc()} call. This interpretation is consistent with the high variability of the mark-follow metric itself. The higher variability of the single-object benchmark relative to the multi-object benchmark follows naturally from its design: concentrating all 20 million weak relations into a single GC cycle amplifies sensitivity to transient system effects such as cache state, NUMA placement, and thread scheduling. The multi-object benchmark spreads the reference load across five cycles, which smooths the observed distributions.

The large advantage of \texttt{weak\_fields} on overall collection metrics stems from eliminating \texttt{WeakReference} wrapper objects entirely. Each \texttt{WeakReference} object occupies heap space for its header, referent, queue, and discovered-list pointer fields~\cite{openjdk-reference-java}, and with 20 million such objects in the single-object benchmark this amounts to approximately 914\,MB of additional live heap. Eliminating these objects reduces GC pressure across all phases: fewer objects to mark, fewer pages to scan during relocation, and less metadata to maintain. The optimised clear path and dynamic array reduce per-reference cost within the processing pipeline, but they cannot offset the broader cost of maintaining 20 million reference objects throughout the GC cycle.

The elevated auxiliary memory of \texttt{clear\_path\_dyn} (1\,268\,MB median in the single-object benchmark) compared to \texttt{all} (884\,MB) reflects an interaction between the optimisations. Without the separate-list mechanism, all queue-less references share a single dynamic array that retains its full allocated capacity throughout the GC cycle. With the separate-list mechanism active, references are split across two independently managed lists, bounding the peak size of each more tightly. In all cases, the additional GC-committed memory is negligible relative to the 100\,GB fixed heap. The identical heap footprints of all \texttt{WeakReference} variants confirm that the optimisations do not affect the number of wrapper objects allocated; only \texttt{weak\_fields}, which eliminates the wrapper objects themselves, reduces Java heap usage.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

Several natural follow-up directions emerge from the results.

First, the queue-less weak-reference optimisations should be evaluated beyond ZGC. The original inefficiency identified in JDK-8029205~\cite{JDK-8029205} is not specific to this thesis, and parts of the idea may transfer to other collectors that also route cleared references through shared enqueue machinery. Such work would need collector-specific design rather than a direct port, because discovery structures, barrier models, and generational policies differ substantially across collectors.

Second, the dynamic-array approach deserves a more detailed memory-performance study. In this thesis it improves non-strong processing time substantially, but at the cost of higher auxiliary memory, especially in the single-object benchmark. Future work should therefore explore alternative growth and retention policies, chunked arrays, or bounded-capacity reuse strategies that preserve locality while reducing peak native-memory commitment.

Third, the weak-field mechanism should be treated as a prototype rather than a finished replacement for \texttt{WeakReference}. The current implementation is intentionally narrow: it focuses on ZGC, old-generation discovery, and a benchmark-driven subset of JVM functionality. A more complete design would need broader compatibility work, a clearer language-level contract, and a fuller correctness evaluation against general Java workloads.

Finally, the current evaluation is intentionally controlled and synthetic. Broader benchmarking on production-style applications, with different heap sizes, allocation patterns, and queue usage ratios, would help determine where the observed gains matter in practice. In particular, workloads that mix queue-less weak references with queue-backed references would clarify how often the specialised fast path can improve real applications rather than stress benchmarks alone.
